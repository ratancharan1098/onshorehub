In-House Medical Billing vs. Outsourcing: How to Compare the Real Costs

  • Hritik Gehlot
  • Nov 15, 2026
  • 6 min read

Most practices compare billing options by looking at one number: the salary of an in-house biller against the fee a billing company charges. That comparison usually leaves out a large part of what in-house billing actually costs, and it says nothing about how much revenue each option collects.

This guide gives you a simple way to compare the two fairly, using your own numbers.

The full cost of in-house billing

Salary is only the starting point. When you add up what it costs to run billing in-house, include:

  • Salary and benefits: wages, health insurance, paid time off, retirement contributions and payroll taxes.
  • Recruiting and training: the time and money spent hiring, plus the weeks before a new biller is fully productive.
  • Software and tools: practice management or billing software, clearinghouse fees, and coding references.
  • Cover for absences: when your biller is sick, on leave or leaves the practice, claims and follow-up can stop.
  • Management time: the hours you or your office manager spend supervising, answering questions and checking the work.
  • Office space and equipment: a desk, computer, phone and other overhead.

Turnover deserves special attention. When a biller leaves, the cost is not only the new hire. Claims go unsubmitted, A/R follow-up stops and knowledge of payer-specific issues leaves with them.

How outsourced billing is usually priced

Billing companies typically charge in one of two ways:

  • A percentage of collections: the fee rises and falls with the money actually collected, which links the billing team's pay to results.
  • A flat monthly fee or dedicated staff model: you pay a fixed amount for a set level of service or for a dedicated person working on your account.

Before you compare quotes, ask exactly what is included. Some companies charge separately for patient statements, credentialing, old A/R cleanup or reporting.

Compare collections, not just costs

The cheapest option is not always the one that leaves you with the most money. A billing setup that costs a little more but collects more of what you are owed can be the better choice. When you compare, look at:

  • Net collection rate: how much of the money you are allowed to collect is actually collected.
  • Days in A/R: how long it takes, on average, to get paid.
  • Denial rate: how many claims are denied on first submission.
  • A/R over 90 days: how much revenue is sitting in older, harder-to-collect claims.

A simple way to run the numbers

Take your last twelve months and work out:

  1. The total annual cost of billing in-house, including every item in the list above.
  2. The total annual cost of the outsourced option, based on your collections and the quote you received.
  3. The difference in expected collections, based on your current net collection rate and A/R, and what the billing partner can realistically improve.

Your real saving or cost is the difference in what you spend, plus the difference in what you collect.

When in-house billing makes sense

In-house billing can work well for practices that have an experienced, stable billing team, enough volume to keep them fully busy, and strong reporting already in place. Some practices also choose a mixed model, keeping a billing lead in-house and outsourcing A/R follow-up, coding or front-desk support.

Questions to ask any billing partner

  • Will you sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA)?
  • How will your staff access our systems, and how is patient data protected?
  • What reports will we receive, and how often?
  • Who will work on our account, and will it be the same people each month?
  • What happens to our data and our open claims if we end the agreement?
