7 Common Reasons Medical Claims Get Denied, and How to Prevent Them

  • Ratan Charan
  • Nov 15, 2026
  • 6 min read

A denied claim is more than a delay. Someone on your team has to find out why it was denied, fix the problem, resubmit it and then wait again for payment. Some denials are never reworked at all, and that revenue is simply lost.

The good news is that most denials come from a short list of causes, and most of those causes can be caught before the claim leaves your office. Here are the seven we see most often when we review a practice's billing, and what you can do about each one.

1. Incorrect or missing patient information

A misspelled name, a wrong date of birth or an outdated member ID is enough for a payer to reject a claim. These errors usually start at the front desk, when details are copied from an old record or taken over the phone.

How to prevent it: Scan the insurance card at every visit, not just the first one, and confirm the patient's details against the card before the appointment is checked in.

2. Coverage or eligibility problems

Patients change jobs, switch plans and lose coverage. If nobody checks eligibility before the visit, you may only find out when the claim comes back denied.

How to prevent it: Verify eligibility for every scheduled patient before the visit, ideally a day or two in advance. Confirm active coverage, the plan type, and whether your provider is in network.

3. Missing prior authorization

Many payers require authorization for imaging, certain procedures, specialty medications and some referrals. Without it, the claim will be denied even if the care was appropriate.

How to prevent it: Keep an up-to-date list of the services that need authorization for each of your main payers, and check it when the appointment is booked, not on the day of the visit. Record the authorization number in the patient's chart so it can be added to the claim.

4. Coding errors

Diagnosis codes that do not support the procedure, missing or incorrect modifiers, and outdated codes are all common reasons for denial. Coding rules change every year, and small mistakes add up.

How to prevent it: Make sure the person coding has current ICD-10-CM, CPT and HCPCS references for your specialty, and review high-value or unusual claims before submission. Regular coding audits will show you where the same mistakes keep happening.

5. Lack of medical necessity documentation

A payer may deny a claim if the documentation does not show why the service was needed. This often happens when the diagnosis on the claim is too general, or when the provider's notes do not match what was billed.

How to prevent it: Code to the highest level of specificity the documentation supports, and give providers simple feedback when their notes are not enough to support what they billed.

6. Duplicate claims

When a claim is resubmitted before the original has been processed, or the same service is entered twice, the second claim is denied as a duplicate. It also makes the account harder to follow.

How to prevent it: Check claim status with the payer before resubmitting anything, and use a corrected claim rather than a new one when you need to change details on a claim that has already been processed.

7. Missed timely filing deadlines

Every payer has a deadline for submitting claims and appeals. Once it passes, the claim can be denied no matter how accurate it is. Deadlines differ by payer and by contract, so they are easy to lose track of.

How to prevent it: Keep a list of filing and appeal deadlines for each payer you bill, submit claims daily rather than in weekly batches, and review your aging report every week so older claims do not slip through.

Track denials so you can fix the cause

Resubmitting denied claims one by one treats the symptom. To bring your denial rate down, log every denial by reason and by payer and review the totals each month. If one reason keeps coming up, the fix usually belongs earlier in the process, at scheduling, registration or coding, rather than in billing.

The cheapest denial to fix is the one that never happens. Most prevention work takes place before the patient is seen, not after the claim is sent.

If you are not sure where your denials are coming from, a billing audit is a good place to start. We review your recent denials, your aging A/R and your front-desk process, and show you where revenue is being lost.
